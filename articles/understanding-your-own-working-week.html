Understanding Your Own Working Week

A patient look at the week you actually work.

Every working week is a personal shape. Yours has its own good hours, its own tolerance for meetings, its own mid-week dip. Colleagues who understand their own week produce better work than colleagues who work against it. This piece is a walk through learning yours.

Every working week is a personal shape

The average working week does not exist. Yours has a specific rhythm — the mornings you concentrate best; the afternoons you fade; the day of the week when meetings feel most useful and the day they feel most draining.

The gap between the average week and yours is where working advice needs to be adapted. Not because the advice is wrong, but because your week is specific.

The morning you actually have

Some people wake sharp at 6am and do their best thinking before 10. Others need a slow start and are at their best from 11 to 2. Others are evening people who never quite come alive before lunch and are still writing thoughtfully at 8pm.

You know which you are. Arrange your calendar so your best thinking hours are not spent in meetings. This is the single most useful working-life adjustment most people can make.

The average working week does not exist. Yours has a specific rhythm, and colleagues who understand their own week produce better work than colleagues who fight against it.

Meeting appetite, honestly measured

How many meetings can you have in a day before you stop being useful in the last one? For most people the honest answer is three or four. After that, the returns fall off sharply.

Notice your number. Then defend it. If you already have four meetings booked, decline the fifth, or move one. Nobody thanks a colleague who was in every meeting but present in none.

The mid-week dip

Most working weeks have a dip. For many people it is Wednesday afternoon; for others Thursday. It is the moment the week feels long and the weekend feels far away.

Plan around it. Do not schedule your hardest meeting for Wednesday at 4pm; put it earlier in the week. Save the dip afternoon for the kind of work you can do while tired — small tidying, catch-up messages, a slow walk-and-think.

Friday afternoon, kindly acknowledged

Friday afternoon is a real thing. Most people cannot start a major piece of thinking work at 3pm on a Friday. Fighting this is a waste of everyone's energy.

Use Friday afternoon for closing things, cleaning things, and setting up the coming Monday. Small tasks; small wins; a clean slate.

A quiet weekly review

Once a week — Friday afternoon works for many people — take twenty minutes to look back at the week. What worked; what did not; what to change next week.

This is not a productivity ritual. It is a small act of noticing. Kept for a quarter, it changes what you attend to. Not kept, and the same patterns repeat unexamined.

What to change, and what to accept

Some things about your week you can change. Meeting-heavy afternoons. A calendar with no focus blocks. A pattern of always saying yes on Monday and always regretting it by Thursday.

Other things you accept and work around. The colleague whose meetings always run long. The company all-hands on the day you were most productive. Acceptance is not defeat; it is the small honest work of doing your job in the week you have.

A short weekly habit

A workable habit: notice your best hours, defend them; count your meeting appetite, respect it; acknowledge the dip; use Friday afternoon to close; twenty minutes of review at the end of each week.

A month of this quietly changes what your week feels like. A quarter of it changes what your year looks like.

In short

  • Notice your good hours and protect them from meetings.
  • Count your honest meeting appetite; defend the number.
  • Plan around the mid-week dip; do not fight it.
  • Use Friday afternoon for closing, not opening.
  • Twenty-minute weekly review, kindly kept.
