How to Give Feedback That Feels Real

Feedback as a small kindness, honestly given.

EWBy Eleanor Whitcombe · Published 8 September 2025 · 10 min read

Feedback is one of the most talked-about and least well-practised parts of workplace life. Too much of it is either delivered so gently that the message never lands, or delivered so bluntly that the relationship it depends on cracks. This piece is about a middle path — feedback that lands because it is specific, timely and honestly given.

Feedback is a small kindness

A colleague who is doing something that is quietly not working, and who no-one has told, is a colleague being failed by their team. Silent tolerance of a problem is not kinder than naming it; it is only quieter.

Good feedback is, at bottom, a small kindness. It is the act of telling someone what you have noticed, so they can decide what to do with the information.

Timing: the day of, or the week of

Feedback lands best when it is close in time to the thing it is about. The day of, if you can. The week of, at the outside. Feedback delivered six weeks later in a performance review has almost no signal — the memory is faded on both sides, and the person cannot easily connect the observation to the specific thing they did.

This does not mean giving feedback in the middle of a meeting. It means booking fifteen minutes the same afternoon, or bringing it up in the next one-to-one on Thursday.

Silent tolerance of a problem is not kinder than naming it; it is only quieter. Feedback, kindly given, is a small act of respect for the person on the other side.

The three-part shape of useful feedback

A useful three-part shape: the specific thing you noticed, the impact you observed, and an invitation. "In yesterday's review meeting, when you interrupted Priya twice — I noticed she did not raise her point again for the rest of the meeting. Could you try leaving more space next time."

That is a whole piece of feedback. Specific, observed, and gently forward-looking. It does not require a framework; it just requires care with the words.

Specific over general, always

General praise is nice but does not change behaviour. "You're doing great" tells the person nothing they can act on. "That summary you sent on Thursday was really useful — the point about scope was exactly the thing the team needed to hear" is a whole different thing.

The same applies to critical feedback. "You need to communicate better" is almost useless. "In the last two team meetings I noticed you didn't share the latest numbers until the last five minutes" is something the person can actually work with.

How to give hard feedback kindly

Book a quiet time. Not the corridor. Not the end of a busy meeting. Book fifteen minutes, in private, when neither of you is rushed.

Start with the observation, plainly. Skip the compliment sandwich; it is transparent and the person hears the middle regardless. Say what you noticed, why it matters, and what you would like to be different. Then be quiet and listen.

How to receive feedback well

When someone gives you feedback, your first job is not to reply. Your first job is to receive it. Say thank you. Ask one clarifying question if you need one. Do not defend, do not explain, do not agree yet.

Sit with it for a day. Then decide what to do with it. Most feedback contains at least a grain of truth; some contains more; a small amount contains none. All of it is worth thinking about before you respond.

What to do with feedback you disagree with

You will receive feedback that you do not agree with. This is normal, and it does not mean the person giving it is wrong; it means the two of you see the situation differently.

Say so honestly, once you have thought about it. "Thank you for that — I've been sitting with it, and I see the moment you mean but I read it differently. Can we talk about it." Feedback is not a verdict; it is the beginning of a conversation.

A short feedback habit

A workable habit: give one specific piece of feedback a week to each person you work closely with. Positive when the moment calls for it; critical when it needs saying. Small, timely, specific.

Over a year, that is forty-five small conversations that would otherwise have happened as one big awkward one, or never. That is the whole trick.

In short

  • Feedback close in time — the day of, or the week of.
  • Specific observation, observed impact, gentle invitation.
  • Skip the compliment sandwich; say the middle plainly.
  • Receiving well means listening first, not replying.
