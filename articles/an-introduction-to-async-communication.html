An Introduction to Async Communication

Writing well when the reply comes later.

MTBy Marcus Tremlett · Published 2 February 2026 · 11 min read

Async communication is not the absence of meetings. It is a craft — writing well when the reply comes later. Teams that learn it get fewer meetings, calmer days and clearer decisions. Teams that do not, tend to fill the gap with more meetings. This piece is a patient introduction.

What async communication actually is

Async communication is any exchange where the reply does not need to be immediate. A written message, a document with comments, a recorded video walk-through, an email. The defining feature is that the responder chooses when to engage.

This is not new. It is how most workplaces used to run before the meeting calendar swallowed the day. What is new is the tools that let async happen at team scale, quickly.

The four kinds of workplace message

Most workplace messages fall into one of four kinds: FYI (no response needed), question (specific answer needed), decision (approval needed), discussion (a conversation to be had).

Label them, at least in your head, before you send. A FYI does not need a reply; treating it like a question adds meetings. A discussion does not fit in a message; treating it like one leaves the conversation half-had.

Async communication is not the absence of meetings. It is a craft — writing well when the reply comes later, and trusting the reader to reply well when the moment is right.

Writing a message the reader can act on

A good async message contains, in this order: what it is about (the subject), what the reader needs to do (the ask), the context (why), and the deadline (when).

"Can I get your review on the draft proposal by Thursday 3pm? Context: the client meeting is Friday morning. Draft attached." Four sentences. The reader knows the ask, the reason and the deadline in ten seconds.

When to reach for a call instead

Async is not always right. Reach for a call when the topic is emotionally charged, when the situation is genuinely urgent, when the number of unknowns is high, or when the relationship is new and needs voice contact.

A useful rule: if the async thread has gone three round-trips without resolving, put a fifteen-minute call on the calendar. The thread was not the right tool for that question.

Response times, agreed in advance

A team should agree, roughly, how quickly it responds to different kinds of messages. Same-day for team channels; next-morning for email; four hours for direct messages during working hours.

These are not rules; they are shared expectations. Having them means nobody is anxious about whether their message was seen, and nobody feels obligated to reply within seven minutes.

Four lines is usually enough

Subject, ask, context, deadline. A message with those four lines is a message the reader can act on in ten seconds. Longer messages are not more polite; they are usually less clear.

The single-thread rule

One topic, one thread. When a new topic appears, start a new thread. This makes the thread searchable, referable, and closable when the topic is resolved.

Threads that contain three topics become impossible to close, and impossible to search. The two-second cost of starting a new thread saves an enormous amount of confusion later.

Notifications, kindly managed

A team that expects everyone to be reachable in real time is not really doing async. Encourage colleagues to close notifications during focus time, and to check them in blocks — every two hours, say.

This is a cultural shift, not a technical one. Managers modelling it — closing their notifications, replying in batches — is worth more than any policy.

A short async habit

A workable habit: when you notice yourself booking a meeting, ask whether a written thread would work. When you write a message, use the four-line shape (subject, ask, context, deadline). When a thread stalls, put a call on the calendar.

A quarter of this and your team's calendar quietly changes shape.

In short

  • Four kinds of message: FYI, question, decision, discussion.
  • Four-line shape: subject, ask, context, deadline.
  • Three round-trips of unresolved thread means put a call on the calendar.
  • Agree team response times so nobody has to guess.
  • One topic, one thread.
