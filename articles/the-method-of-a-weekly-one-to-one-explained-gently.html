The Method of a Weekly One-to-One, Explained Gently

The most useful ritual in a working life.

EWBy Eleanor Whitcombe · Published 11 August 2025 · 10 min read

The weekly one-to-one is a small ritual with an outsized effect. Thirty minutes, every week, between a manager and a direct report, kept consistently across months and years. On paper it looks like an ordinary meeting. In practice it is one of the most useful rituals in a working life. This piece is about how to run one that is worth the half hour.

Why the weekly one-to-one earns its half hour

A one-to-one is not a status update. It is the small standing appointment where two people who work together can talk about anything at all — the current work, the current worries, the shape of the coming month — without needing an excuse.

The point is not the specific half hour. The point is that the half hour exists, reliably, so that when something needs saying, both people know when to say it. Almost every good working relationship rests, quietly, on this predictability.

Setting a time and keeping it

Book the one-to-one for the same day and time every week. Tuesday at 3pm, Friday at 11am — the specific slot matters less than its unchanging quality. Both people arrange the rest of their week around it.

Do not cancel one-to-ones lightly. If the manager cancels three weeks running, the meeting stops meaning anything, and the direct report quietly stops preparing for it. If a week is genuinely impossible, move it — do not skip it.

Whose meeting it actually is

A one-to-one is the direct report's meeting, not the manager's. Their agenda, their topics, their questions. The manager's job is to prepare good questions and then largely to listen.

This inverts what many managers assume. If you find yourself doing most of the talking in a one-to-one, you have accidentally turned it into a status update or a lecture. Hand the room back.

The three quiet parts of a one-to-one

A workable shape is three parts: how is the work, how are you, what is coming up. The first is about the current work; the second is about the person; the third is about the near future.

You do not need to hit all three every week. Some weeks the whole meeting is about one difficult project. Other weeks it is fifteen minutes of catching up and out early. Both are fine.

Questions that open the room

A useful set of questions to keep in your back pocket: what has been on your mind this week; what feels harder than it should; what would you like more or less of from me; what is one thing I could do differently.

You will not ask all of these every week. But having a few in mind means that when the direct report says "nothing much to report", you have a way to open the room a little further.

The direct report's meeting

A one-to-one belongs to the direct report. Their agenda, their topics. If the manager is doing most of the talking, the meeting has quietly become something else.

What does not belong in a one-to-one

Status updates that could have been an email do not belong in a one-to-one. Neither does a large performance discussion — that should have its own meeting, prepared for. Neither does venting about a third colleague as a substitute for talking to them.

When the current work has crowded out everything else for three weeks running, the manager should notice and gently make room. "Let's park the project for ten minutes — how are you actually doing."

Notes, kindly kept

A shared document — one per direct report, kept by both people — is the friendliest way to hold notes. Both add topics through the week; both look back at previous notes; both can see the arc of what has been discussed.

This is not surveillance. It is the small kindness of not having to remember, and of being able to refer back to what was agreed three weeks ago without asking the other person to remember for you.

A short one-to-one habit

A workable habit: set the standing slot, book it into the next twelve weeks at once, prepare with three lines of notes before each session, and keep the meeting even in weeks that feel light.

Over a year, that is roughly forty-five conversations. Almost every working relationship that works well is the sum of forty-five ordinary conversations, kept honestly.

In short

  • Same slot every week; kept even in light weeks.
  • The direct report owns the agenda, not the manager.
  • Three parts: how is the work, how are you, what is coming up.
  • Keep a shared running document of notes.
