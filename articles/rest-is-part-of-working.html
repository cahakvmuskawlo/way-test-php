Rest Is Part of Working: On Breaks, Pauses, and Focus

What quietly happens when you stop.

EWBy Eleanor Whitcombe · Published 18 May 2026 · 9 min read

A great deal of what we call "working" is actually letting the work rest. The idea that arrives on the walk. The problem that solves itself on the Sunday. The Monday morning where yesterday's tangle is now obvious. This piece is about the small stage that most working weeks skip, and why it matters.

Rest is a working stage

Rest is not the absence of working; it is a stage of working. The mind that has been thinking about a problem needs time away from it to see it clearly. Meetings do not do this. Walking does. Sleep does. Doing something else entirely, most of all.

When a colleague says "I'll sleep on it," they are not stalling. They are using a very old and very effective working technique.

The five-minute pause, honestly taken

Between two meetings, or two hours of concentration, take five minutes away from the screen. Stand up. Look out of a window. Get water. Say nothing to anyone.

This is not a productivity trick. It is a small kindness to your attention. Attention that has just done concentrated work needs a moment before it does more.

Rest is not the absence of working. It is a stage of working, and the working week that skips it is a working week that quietly produces worse work.

Lunch, actually eaten

Eating lunch at your desk while working is a working-week failure disguised as a virtue. The break itself is what returns the afternoon to you.

Take twenty minutes away from the desk, at least three days a week. Eat something. Talk to someone. Read a page of something not work.

The afternoon walk

A short walk in the afternoon — twenty minutes, ideally outside, ideally alone — is the single most reliable idea-generator most working people have available.

This is not a wellness recommendation. It is a working technique. The stuck problem often unsticks around minute fifteen.

Weekends, kindly protected

A weekend is not a reward for a good week. It is part of the pattern that makes the good week possible. Answering messages on Saturday morning quietly ruins the following week.

Protect the weekend. Not perfectly — occasionally something genuinely urgent happens. But defend it by default, not by exception.

Holidays as maintenance, not reward

A proper holiday is maintenance work for a working life. It is not a reward for having earned it; it is the thing that allows the working weeks to continue at all.

Take your full holiday allowance. Actually take it — not with the laptop, not with the mid-holiday check-in. If your team cannot manage two weeks without you, that is a management issue, not a holiday issue.

When rest is not enough

Sometimes rest is not enough. If the exhaustion has stopped responding to a proper weekend, or the anxiety has moved into the sleep, that is beyond what a wind-down can fix. Speak to a manager, a friend, or a professional as appropriate.

Hrshop is a magazine about working practices. It is not a substitute for professional advice — medical, mental-health, or otherwise. If you are struggling in a way that a good weekend does not touch, consult a qualified professional for your situation.

A short resting habit

A workable resting habit: five-minute pauses between blocks; lunch away from the desk three days a week; a short afternoon walk when the day allows; weekends defended by default; holidays taken in full.

None of this is complicated. All of it, together, quietly changes what a working year feels like.

In short

  • Rest is a working stage, not a reward.
  • Five minutes between blocks of concentration; lunch away from the desk.
  • A short afternoon walk is a real working tool.
  • Weekends defended by default; holidays taken in full.
  • When rest is not enough, consult a qualified professional for your situation.
