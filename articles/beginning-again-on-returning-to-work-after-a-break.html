Beginning Again: On Returning to Work After a Break

The gentle work of starting again.

EWBy Eleanor Whitcombe · Published 6 July 2026 · 10 min read

People fall out of the working week for many reasons — illness, a move, a hard year, a child, a bereavement, a career pause. Coming back to work is a small quiet act, and it deserves a small quiet welcome. This piece is about returning to work without shame.

A quiet return, without ceremony

Most people, over a life, have periods when they do not work. This is normal. It is not a moral failing. Illness, care work, exhaustion, grief, a hard year — all of these can quietly take working weeks away from a person.

What matters is not whether you stopped. What matters is that you are starting again. That is a small good thing.

What has changed since you last worked

The tools have changed. The team has probably changed. The vocabulary has probably changed. The industry may have moved on in ways that make your previous knowledge feel slightly out of date.

This is normal, and it is smaller than it looks from outside. Most of what you knew still applies. Some of it has moved. The learning is on the surface, not in the substance.

Coming back to work is not a project. It is a small quiet act, done softly, at your own pace, without shame.

What has not changed at all

What has not changed: how to have a one-to-one; how to give feedback; how to run a small team meeting; how to write a clear message; how to be a person another person can trust to close the loop.

These are the skills that carry across every workplace and every pause. You have not lost them, whatever the last few months have felt like.

A first four weeks, gently sized

A workable first four weeks: week one is arriving; week two is meeting; week three is a small piece of real work; week four is a proper conversation with your manager about what next.

Do not ask more of yourself than this. Do not compare yourself to the version of you before the break. The first month is a beginning.

The old habits that still work

A short good-morning to the team. A weekly one-to-one with your manager. A quiet Friday afternoon to close the week. Written notes after decisions. Specific thank-yous when a colleague does something worth thanking.

These are the habits that worked before, and they still work. Return to them.

The new habits worth adding

A short daily walk. A twenty-minute weekly review. An honest count of your meeting appetite. A conscious wind-down at the end of the day.

These are the habits that a returning working life is a good moment to add. You are already changing your shape; add the shape you want.

What to expect at three months

By three months back, you will feel roughly like a working person again. Not exactly like the person before; you have been changed by whatever caused the pause. But recognisably a working person, with a working week, in a working team.

This is not fast. Three months is the honest timeline. If you are still feeling shaky at four or five, that is also normal and worth talking about with your manager or a friend.

A steady, unspectacular working life

The ambition after a return is not brilliance. It is steadiness. Do the small things well; keep the small habits; close the loops; be a person another person can rely on.

A steady, unspectacular working life is a very good working life. It is what most of us are actually trying to build. It is enough.

In short

  • Falling out of working weeks happens for many honest reasons.
  • Most of what you knew still applies; the learning is on the surface.
  • A first four weeks, gently sized: arriving, meeting, small work, conversation.
  • Return to the old habits that worked; add a few new ones kindly.
  • Nothing here is legal or professional HR advice; consult a qualified professional for your situation.
