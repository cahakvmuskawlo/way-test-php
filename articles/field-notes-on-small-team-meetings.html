Field Notes on Small Team Meetings

Six or eight people around a table, done kindly.

MTBy Marcus Tremlett · Published 6 October 2025 · 11 min read

The small team meeting — six or eight people around a table, or in a video call, for an hour — is one of the most common and least examined rituals of workplace life. Done well, it is the small standing appointment where the team knows itself. Done badly, it is the reason nobody trusts their calendar. This piece is about the friendly end.

What a small team meeting is for

A small team meeting is for the things that need everyone in the room at the same time. Decisions that need collective input. Updates where the discussion matters, not the transmission. The small moments of alignment that only happen when people can look at each other.

Almost everything else — status, information, straightforward decisions — belongs in writing. If a meeting could have been an email, the email would probably have been better.

An agenda that fits on one screen

A workable agenda for a small team meeting fits on one screen. Three or four topics; ten to fifteen minutes each; a name against each item as the person who owns it.

Share the agenda before the meeting, ideally the day before, so people can turn up with the small amount of thinking they need to have done. This is not process for its own sake; it is respect for the hour.

A meeting whose actions never get written down is a meeting that will need to be re-had in three weeks. The five minutes of writing at the end saves the whole future meeting.

Who talks, and when

A meeting where the same two people talk for most of the hour is a meeting that has failed to include the others. The chair's job is to notice this and gently open the room. "Priya, you've been thinking about this — what do you see?"

This is not about equal airtime as a rule. It is about the room being genuinely available to everyone in it.

The pause after a question

When somebody asks a real question in a meeting, the answer often comes from an unexpected voice. But only if the chair gives it room. Five seconds of silence, kindly held, produces different answers than one second.

Meetings that fill every silence with words end up with the same three voices talking. Meetings that hold small silences make room for the sixth voice.

When to end a meeting early

End the meeting when its work is done. Not at the top of the hour because the calendar said so. Fifteen minutes given back to the team is more generous than any polished summary.

The unspoken rule in most workplaces is that meetings run for their full booked time. Break the rule kindly and consistently, and after a few weeks people notice, and they trust the meeting more.

The quiet person, kindly included

Every team has at least one member who is quieter in the group than they are one-to-one. This is not a flaw to be fixed; it is a person to be included differently.

Ask them by name for their view on a specific topic, ideally one you know they have thought about. "Ade, you've been in this before — what did you see last time?" Do this without making a small ceremony of it. Include them, quietly.

Notes and follow-ups

Every meeting that ends with a decision or an action should have those decisions and actions written down, with names, within an hour of ending. Not a full transcript — just the decisions, the owners, and the next dates.

A meeting whose actions never get written down is a meeting that will need to be re-had in three weeks. The five minutes of writing at the end saves that whole future meeting.

A short team-meeting habit

A workable habit: agenda shared the day before, chair rotates monthly, actions written within an hour, meeting ends when its work is done. Nothing else.

Over a quarter, this quietly changes what a team's calendar feels like — and what the team feels like inside its meetings.

In short

  • Agenda on one screen, shared the day before.
  • Chair notices who has not spoken, and opens the room.
  • Hold small silences after real questions.
  • Decisions and actions written down within an hour.
  • End the meeting when its work is done, not when the clock says so.
