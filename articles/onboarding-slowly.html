Onboarding, Slowly

The first month of a new job, considered patiently.

EWBy Eleanor Whitcombe · Published 14 July 2025 · 12 min read

Onboarding is not an event, it is a season. In too many teams it gets treated as a checklist to complete in the first two days: laptop, badge, welcome slide deck, HR system, and back to your normal week. What actually happens over the first ninety days of a new job is much more consequential, and much slower, than any checklist can capture. This piece is about giving that season the room it deserves.

Onboarding is a season, not an event

The first ninety days of a new job are a working season with a shape of its own. The new person is trying to learn who does what, how decisions are actually made, where the useful documents live, which meetings matter, and which relationships they need to build. The team is trying to fold a new voice into the room without losing what already worked. Neither can be rushed.

What breaks in most onboardings is not the paperwork. It is the pace. The new person is treated as somebody who should be productive by week two, when in fact they are still building the map they need in order to be productive at all. A team that expects real output in the first month gets performed output; a team that expects real output in the third month gets it.

The week before the new person arrives

The week before the new person's first day is the quietest and most important part of onboarding. It is when the team does the small work that makes day one hospitable rather than chaotic.

Order the laptop early enough that it arrives before they do. Book the accounts they need across the systems they'll use. Write a short, specific document — half a page — describing what the person will do, who they'll work with, and what the first two weeks are meant to look like. Share this document with the team, so that the new person is not being explained to every colleague from scratch on Monday morning.

Assign a buddy — someone junior enough to remember what it felt like to be new, senior enough to answer questions without checking three times. Tell the buddy in advance so it does not arrive as a Monday-morning surprise.

Day one, honestly

Day one is not the day to accomplish anything. It is the day to arrive. A humane day-one shape looks something like this: a proper introduction to the manager over an hour, not a rushed twenty minutes; lunch with the buddy or a small group; a walk through the office, or a proper video introduction to the team; time to set up the machine without rush; and an early finish, because the first day is exhausting and there is nothing to be gained by pushing through.

Do not schedule the new person into six meetings on their first day. Do not ask them to present themselves to the whole company at 4pm. Do not send them home with reading to complete before Tuesday. Day one is a beginning, not a proof of anything.

The first week: small wins, small maps

The first week is for small maps. Who sits where; which channels the team uses; what the weekly rhythm looks like; where the important documents live. The new person is building an internal atlas, and every conversation they have is a small pin on that atlas.

Give them one small piece of real work in the first week — small enough to finish, real enough to matter, related enough to their eventual role to be worth doing. A well-scoped first task is one of the kindest things a team can offer. It says: we trust you enough to hand you something real, and small enough that you cannot fail dramatically at it.

The first month: rhythms and rituals

By the end of the first month, the new person should have found the working rhythm of the team. They should know what a normal Monday looks like, when the recurring meetings happen, and roughly what the team is working on that quarter. They should not yet be shipping large pieces of work independently. They should be contributing in small, honest ways.

The manager's job through the first month is to make sure the rhythms exist and are legible. Weekly one-to-ones from day one, even if there is not much to talk about yet. Predictable team meetings with a small agenda. Small, honest introductions to the people they will work with.

What a buddy actually does

A buddy is not a mentor. A buddy is the person the new colleague can ask the small stupid questions — where is the printer, how do I book leave, does everyone eat lunch at their desks or is that just the Monday people. A buddy is a low-stakes local guide.

Good buddies are usually a level or two above the newcomer, ideally in an adjacent team so the questions are safe. They should have a short weekly coffee with the newcomer for the first month or so, and then let the relationship become whatever it becomes. The buddy is a bridge, not a chaperone.

Ninety days, kindly

Set an explicit ninety-day check-in on the calendar during the first week. Both sides know it is coming, and both sides can bring honest observations to it. The knowledge that the check-in exists changes the quality of the first three months.

Feedback in the first ninety days

Feedback in the first ninety days should flow both ways. The manager gives small, specific feedback in the weekly one-to-one — what is going well, what to try next, where they saw a small stumble. Nothing dramatic. Nothing performance-review shaped.

And the manager asks for feedback in return. What has surprised you? What was harder to figure out than it should have been? What would you change about your first weeks? The new person, who is currently seeing the team with fresh eyes, will not see the team this clearly again. This is the moment to hear what the team looks like from outside.

Write down the answers. Every small observation is a note for how to onboard the next person better.

The first ninety days of a new job are not for proving anything. They are for building the map, small piece by small piece, that makes real work possible in the year that follows.

A short onboarding habit

If any of this feels like a lot, the shortest possible version is this: prepare the week before, give day one air, offer one small real task in the first week, keep weekly one-to-ones from the start, assign a buddy for small questions, and hold a proper ninety-day check-in for feedback in both directions.

None of those are HR programmes. All of them are small kindnesses done consistently. Over a year, they quietly change what it feels like to be new on your team, and quietly change how long people stay.

In short

  • Onboarding is a ninety-day season, not a two-day checklist.
  • Prepare the week before: laptop, accounts, a half-page brief, a buddy.
  • Give day one air; avoid stacking meetings or presentations.
  • Offer one small, honest, finishable piece of work in the first week.
  • Weekly one-to-ones from day one, small and predictable.
  • Ninety-day check-in in both directions, honestly kept.
