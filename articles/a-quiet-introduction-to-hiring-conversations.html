A Quiet Introduction to Hiring Conversations

A patient walk through interviews that respect the person on the other side.

EWBy Eleanor Whitcombe · Published 16 June 2025 · 12 min read

There is a particular kind of small hiring failure that only happens because the interviewer did not prepare. The candidate arrives on time; the hiring manager arrives frazzled from the previous meeting; the first ten minutes are lost to finding a working room; and by the time anyone has settled, the interview is halfway over. Nobody in the room quite manages to show themselves. Nobody quite gets to see them. This piece is about the small kindnesses that prevent that, and about how to have a hiring conversation that respects the person on the other side of the desk.

Why hiring conversations deserve care

A job interview is an odd kind of meeting. Both people are trying to decide whether to spend a substantial portion of their next several years together. Neither has enough information. One is nervous; the other is often distracted. And the entire exchange is compressed into forty-five minutes on a Tuesday afternoon, sandwiched between an all-hands and a lunch.

Given that, the surprising thing is not that many hiring conversations go badly. The surprising thing is that any of them go well. When they do, it is almost always because the interviewer treated the meeting as a real thing that deserved preparation, rather than as an interruption to be managed.

Before the interview: what the job actually is

Most hiring failures start before the interview room. They start with a job description that nobody on the hiring team quite agrees about. Two of the four interviewers think the role is about a certain kind of steady execution; the other two think it is about setting direction. Both are trying to decide whether the same candidate is the right person, and they are asking about different jobs.

Half an hour, a week before the first candidate arrives, is worth ten hours of interviewing later. Sit with the small hiring team. Agree, on paper, what the person will actually do in their first ninety days. Agree what a good outcome looks like a year in. Agree what the person absolutely needs to be able to do on day one, and what they can learn on the job. If the group cannot agree, the group is not ready to interview yet.

The first ten minutes, honestly

The first ten minutes of an interview are almost entirely about settling the room. A candidate who has spent thirty minutes on the tube, or two hours on a video-call setup, is not yet the version of themselves you are trying to meet. Give them a few minutes of ordinary conversation before you begin. Ask about the journey; offer water; explain how the meeting is going to run.

This is not small talk in a bad sense. It is a kindness. It also, quietly, gives you information about how the person handles unstructured conversation, which is a real thing you want to know before you spend an hour asking structured questions.

Questions that let a person show themselves

The most useful interview questions are the ones that let the person on the other side of the desk show you something specific about how they work. Not "where do you see yourself in five years," which nobody has an honest answer to. Not "what is your greatest weakness," which everyone has practised.

Instead: tell me about a piece of work you finished last month that you are proud of, and walk me through it. Tell me about a time you disagreed with a decision your team was making, and what you did about it. Tell me what you found hardest about your last role, in the honest version rather than the interview version.

These questions do two things. They let the candidate speak from specifics rather than abstractions, which is where you actually learn something. And they let the candidate be a real person with a real work history, rather than a performance of a candidate.

Listening more than you talk

The single most common mistake in an interview is that the interviewer talks too much. It feels, in the moment, like being generous — filling silences, explaining the company, helping the candidate along. It is usually the opposite of useful. The interviewer already knows what they know. The purpose of the meeting is to find out what the candidate knows.

A reasonable target: the candidate should be talking about seventy per cent of the time, the interviewer about thirty. When you notice yourself talking more than that, stop mid-sentence and hand the room back with a question. The good interviewers hold their questions loosely and let the answers wander a bit before circling back.

The awkward pause, kindly held

A useful skill for anyone running interviews is holding a pause. When a candidate finishes an answer, wait five seconds before speaking. Not to test them; to let them add the thing they were about to add. The most honest sentence in an interview is often the one that comes after the pause, when the candidate realises they still have room and decides to say the harder version of what they were saying.

This is uncomfortable at first. It gets easier. It also, quietly, changes the quality of the answers you receive.

A small kindness

Keep a glass of water on the candidate's side of the table and refill it yourself, quietly, halfway through. It is a small hospitable gesture, and it also gives the candidate a natural pause to breathe.

After the interview: notes, decisions, and time

Write your notes within an hour of the interview ending. Not later that day. Not the next morning. Within an hour, while the specifics are fresh. Note what the candidate said, in their words where you can remember them. Note where you felt uncertain and where you felt convinced.

Do not decide immediately. Sleep on it, at least one night. The candidate you are excited about at 5pm on Tuesday is not always the candidate you are excited about at 9am on Wednesday, and vice versa. Most hiring decisions improve for a small overnight rest.

When the small hiring team meets to decide, share notes before opinions. Write them down first, share them, then discuss. This prevents the loudest voice from anchoring the conversation, and gives the quieter interviewers a fair chance to say what they saw.

An interview is not an examination. It is an unusually formal first conversation between two adults who are both trying to work out whether the answer is yes.

A short habit for hiring managers

If any of this feels like a lot, the shortest possible version is this: prepare the role on paper before the first candidate arrives; treat the first ten minutes of every interview as hospitality rather than assessment; ask questions that require specifics; talk about a third of the time; hold your pauses; write your notes within the hour; and sleep on your decision.

None of that is complicated. None of it requires training. All of it, together, quietly changes what your hiring looks like from the candidate's side — and, over a year, changes who says yes to your offers.

In short

  • Agree what the job actually is before the first candidate arrives.
  • Treat the first ten minutes as hospitality, not assessment.
  • Ask questions that require specific stories, not abstract answers.
  • Listen about seventy per cent of the time; talk about thirty.
  • Hold a five-second pause after each answer; the honest sentence often comes there.
  • Write notes within an hour; sleep on the decision at least one night.
